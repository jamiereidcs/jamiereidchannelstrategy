Are You Enabling the Right Person at Your Partner?

Most enablement programs train one person at the partner and wonder why the pipeline never shows up. The seller opens the door, the sales engineer decides whether you walk through it, and the handoff between them is where deals quietly disappear.

Jamie Reid November 2026 6 min read

Most partner enablement programs rest on a quiet assumption: if we train the partner, the partner will sell. But "the partner" isn't a person. It's a seller who decides whether your product comes up at all, a sales engineer who decides whether it survives the evaluation, and a handoff between the two that most vendors never think about.

I've watched programs pour budget into one of those people and then wonder why the pipeline never showed up. The usual diagnosis is that they enabled the wrong person. The real problem is that they only enabled one.

The Seller Opens the Door

Sellers own the moment of mention. They're in the renewal call, the QBR, the hallway conversation where a customer says something that should make your product come to mind. If it doesn't come to mind, nothing else in your program matters.

Here's the thing: sellers don't need product depth. They need triggers. Three questions to ask. One customer story they can tell from memory. A clear signal for when to pull in their SE.

Most seller enablement fails because it tries to turn the rep into a junior SE. Forty-five minutes of features, architecture, and competitive positioning. The rep retains almost none of it, and worse, now they're nervous about getting a technical question wrong in front of a customer. So they don't bring it up. You've trained them right out of mentioning you.

The Sales Engineer Decides Whether You Walk Through It

The SE is the trust broker. When the customer turns and asks "what would you do here?", the SE answers. In security especially, a recommendation from the technical person who has actually used the product carries far more weight than a pitch.

And SEs are wildly underserved. Vendors aim SPIFFs, contests, and slick decks at sellers. SEs get a datasheet and a demo login, if they're lucky.

SEs won't recommend something they haven't touched. They need hands-on access: a sandbox, labs built around real scenarios, and a direct line to your engineers when something breaks. Earn that, and the payoff compounds. Once an SE builds you into a reference architecture or a standard offering, you start showing up in deals you never touched.

Complexity Moved the Recommendation to the SE

Products used to be easier to slot in. One problem, one product, one SKU. A seller could match the customer's pain to the product and be mostly right.

That's not the world we sell in anymore. Products overlap, integrate, and depend on each other. A customer's problem rarely maps to a single tool. It maps to a stack. Email security is a good example: authentication, enforcement, inbound threat protection, and brand indicators all touch each other, and the right answer depends on what the customer already runs.

When the problem gets that layered, the seller can usually tell something is wrong, but they can't say what fits. The SE can. They see the customer's environment, understand where the gaps are, and know how your product works alongside what's already there. That makes them the person who positions you as part of a solution instead of one more product to evaluate.

This changes what SE enablement has to cover. Knowing your product isn't enough anymore. SEs need to know where you fit in the stack: what you integrate with, what you replace, what you complement, and which customer scenarios make you the obvious answer. Give them reference architectures and better-together scenarios with the technology partners they already sell. An SE who can draw your product into the customer's architecture diagram is worth more than ten who can recite your feature list.

It also changes the seller's job, and not the way most vendors think. As products get more complex, the seller's job gets simpler: recognize the problem pattern and bring the SE in early. The worst outcome is a seller trying to explain a complex solution alone and losing the customer in the details.

What Happens When You Only Enable One

Enable only the seller, and you get mentions without momentum. Registrations come in, then stall at technical evaluation because the SE has never seen your product and won't stake their credibility on it. After a few of those, the rep quietly stops leading with you. Why keep pitching something that keeps dying?

Enable only the SE, and you get the opposite: a technical champion with no invitations. Your SE contact knows the product cold, but the seller never surfaces the opportunity, so the SE never gets the chance to recommend it. Pipeline stays quiet, and the few deals that do appear close at a great rate.

On a dashboard, both of these look like a partner problem. Both are actually enablement design problems.

Enable the Handoff, Not Just the People

The most overlooked piece of partner enablement is the moment the seller hands the opportunity to the SE. That's where deals leak.

Give both roles a shared language: the same trigger questions, the same qualification criteria, the same customer story. The seller knows what to listen for and exactly what to say to bring the SE in. The SE knows what the seller already promised and picks up from there instead of starting over.

A few things that work in practice:

  • Joint sessions where the seller and SE from the same partner go through enablement together
  • A one-page handoff guide: what the seller says, what the SE does next
  • Deal reviews with both roles in the room, not just the rep
  • Incentives for both. If your SPIFF only pays the seller, the SE has no reason to prioritize your product over the one they already know

This is also why I build separate sales and technical certification tracks. Same product, same story, different jobs.

The MSP Version of This Problem

In managed service providers, the roles blur. The "seller" might be a vCIO, an account manager running quarterly business reviews, or a technician who spots a misconfiguration and flags it. Sometimes that technician is the best seller you have and doesn't know it.

Before you build a single piece of content, map who actually plays which role at each partner. Ask it directly during onboarding: who brings new solutions to customers, and who validates them? The answer changes what you build.

Measure Both Sides Separately

If you track enablement as one number, you'll never see which side is broken. Split it:

  • Seller side: opportunities surfaced and deals registered
  • SE side: technical win rate and time from registration to close

High registrations with a low close rate usually points to an SE gap. Low registrations with a strong win rate usually points to a seller gap. Then compare partners where both roles are certified against partners where only one is. That comparison is where the real story lives.

Ask Which People, Not How Many

The next time you look at your enablement numbers, don't stop at how many people at a partner got certified. Ask which people. A partner with ten certified sellers and no technical champion isn't enabled. Neither is one brilliant SE nobody ever invites to the call.

Enable the seller to open the door. Enable the SE to walk the customer through it. Then enable the handoff, because that's where most of your deals are quietly disappearing.
